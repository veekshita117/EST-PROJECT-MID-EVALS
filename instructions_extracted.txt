Abstract

29
30

Understanding how vegetation phenology responds to climatic variability and topographic heterogeneity is critical

31

for assessing forest resilience yet remains poorly quantified in tropical deciduous systems. We analysed

32

phenological dynamics in Bori Wildlife Sanctuary, central India, using multi-sensor satellite data (Sentinel-2,

33

Landsat-8, MODIS) from 2018 to 2024. GPR-smoothed NDVI and EVI time series across five complete July–

34

June phenological years were used to derive seasonal metrics, including Start of Season (SOS), Peak of Season

35

(POS), End of Season (EOS), and growing season length, together with greenness metrics including seasonal

36

amplitude and the seasonal Area Under the Curve (AUC), alongside a CHIRPS rainfall climatology referenced to

37

the 1991–2020 normal. Green-up occurred in late May (mean calendar DOY 150), some five weeks before

38

monsoon onset, and the landscape-mean growing season averaged 279 days. Topography controlled the amplitude

39

of the seasonal cycle but not its timing: amplitude declined monotonically from valleys to steep slopes and from

40

low to high elevation in all eight sensor–index comparisons (Sentinel-2 NDVI, elevation rho = −0.79, corrected p

41

= 0.024), while start of season varied by under three days across the full elevation gradient and the seasonal

42

integral did not decline. No phenological year reached the −19% rainfall deficit defining a deficient monsoon, so

43

drought response could not be tested; the clearest interannual signal was instead a rise in dry-season baseline

44

greenness of 0.06–0.11 NDVI over the final two years, reproduced by all three sensors and uncorrelated with

45

monsoon rainfall. Cross-sensor agreement in start of season was strongest between Landsat-8 and Sentinel-2 (rho

46

= 0.98). These findings highlight the value of multi-sensor phenology for detecting climate-driven vegetation

47

dynamics and supporting adaptive forest management.

1. Introduction

58

Understanding the timing of plant life-cycle events such as leaf emergence, flowering, and senescence is

59

central to evaluating vegetation growth cycles, seasonal vegetation dynamics, and responses to environmental

60

change (Tecuapetla-Gómez et al. 2024; Yu et al. 2024). Phenology is widely regarded as one of the most sensitive

61

indicators of ecosystem health and serves as an early warning system for climate change impacts (Piao et al. 2019;

62

Caparros-Santiago et al. 2021). Across ecosystems, shifts in phenological events such as earlier green-up,

63

prolonged growing seasons, and delayed senescence signal altered climatic conditions, with implications for

64

biodiversity, ecosystem services, and agriculture (Menzel et al. 2006; Piao et al. 2019; Hassan et al. 2024). Such

65

changes disrupt species interactions (Visser et al. 2006; Hughes et al. 2000), alter carbon and water cycles, and

66

increase vulnerability to invasive species and disease outbreaks. However, attributing these shifts to specific

67

drivers remains challenging because phenology reflects the combined influence of large-scale climatic forcing

68

(e.g., temperature and rainfall variability) and local controls such as topography, soils, and microclimate

69

(McNichol et al. 2024). Disentangling these interacting influences is therefore critical for interpreting observed

70

phenological variability and for distinguishing climate-driven responses from terrain-mediated effects.

71

Historically, phenology was studied through field observations, which provided valuable detail but lacked spatial

72

and temporal coverage (Laurin et al. 2024). The advent of remote sensing transformed the field by enabling

73

consistent, multi-decadal monitoring of vegetation phenology across scales (Gong et al. 2024). Satellite-derived

74

vegetation indices such as the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) and Enhanced Vegetation Index

75

(EVI) are now fundamental tools for quantifying seasonal dynamics across ecosystems ranging from croplands

76

(Wu et al. 2024) to forests (Singh et al. 2023; Myneni et al. 1997). NDVI is a well-established proxy for canopy

77

greenness used to characterise seasonal vegetation dynamics (Pettorelli et al. 2005) and is widely applied to detect

78

the start (SOS), peak (POS), and end of the season (EOS) (Singh et al. 2023). EVI extends these capabilities by

79

reducing saturation in dense canopies and accounting for soil and atmospheric effects, making it particularly

80

valuable in tropical forests (Huete et al. 2002; Zhang et al. 2006). However, satellite-derived phenological metrics

81

in tropical deciduous forests are subject to known uncertainties. Variations in NDVI/EVI may not always directly

82

correspond to actual phenophases due to lag effects associated with canopy structure, leaf area index (LAI), and

83

the presence of non-photosynthetic vegetation. Previous studies have reported that remotely sensed phenology

84

can deviate from field observations in structurally complex canopies, although overall seasonal trajectories remain

85

closely related to ecosystem-level vegetation dynamics and moisture availability (Zhang et al. 2006; Laurin et al.

86

2024). In data-limited regions such as central India, where long-term ground phenology records are scarce, studies

3

87

often rely on consistency assessments such as cross-sensor agreement, consistency across vegetation indices, and

88

ecological coherence with known climatic patterns. While such approaches cannot establish agreement with field-

89

observed phenology, they provide a pragmatic basis for assessing the consistency of remotely sensed phenological

90

metrics (Pettorelli et al. 2005; Caparros-Santiago et al. 2021). No field phenology observations were available for

91

the study area, therefore, agreement across independent sensors and vegetation indices was used here as an indirect

92

assessment of metric reliability rather than absolute validation (Section 3.5).

93

Recent advances in multi-sensor datasets have further strengthened phenological monitoring by integrating

94

observations across spatial and temporal resolutions. MODIS, with its global 16-day composites, has been pivotal

95

for long-term phenological monitoring at coarse resolution (Zhang et al. 2003; Zhang et al. 2006). Landsat-8 offers

96

medium-resolution coverage suitable for landscape-scale analysis (Roy et al. 2014), while Sentinel-2 provides

97

high-resolution reflectance data with frequent revisit intervals, allowing fine-scale monitoring of heterogeneous

98

ecosystems (Phiri et al. 2020). These sensors complement one another, allowing cross-sensor comparison of

99

metrics and improved detection of spatial and temporal variability. Harmonised multi-sensor products have made

100

such integration operational at continental scale (Claverie et al. 2018; Bolton et al. 2020), and comparisons across

101

four observational scales from 3 m PlanetScope to near-surface cameras, show that retrieved transition dates are

102

themselves scale-dependent rather than a fixed property of the canopy (Moon et al. 2021). Nevertheless,

103

integrating multi-sensor datasets introduces challenges related to spatial resolution differences, mixed pixel

104

effects, and varying data availability under cloud-prone conditions. Coarse-resolution sensors may smooth fine-

105

scale heterogeneity, while high-resolution sensors are more sensitive to local variability and data gaps (Moreno-

106

Martínez et al. 2020). In addition, differences in sensor coverage across terrain gradients and cloud contamination

107

during the monsoon season can introduce uneven sampling, potentially affecting phenology extraction (Peng et

108

al. 2021). Addressing these uncertainties requires explicit cross-sensor comparison, careful preprocessing, and

109

transparent interpretation of scale-dependent differences (Gong et al. 2024). These constraints are sharpest in the

110

humid and monsoonal tropics, where no single sensor simultaneously delivers fine spatial resolution, frequent

111

revisit and low cloud contamination, making observational capability, rather than canopy behaviour, a key

112

determinant of what can be detected (Nagai et al. 2023). Multiscale comparisons in seasonally dry tropical forests

113

confirm that transition dates retrieved at different scales diverge systematically along aridity gradients (Ramos et

114

al. 2023).

115

Despite these advances, three gaps remain. First, few studies have systematically integrated multi-sensor data to

116

examine phenological variability in tropical deciduous forests, particularly in India (e.g., Nanda et al. 2014;

4

117

Christian et al. 2015). Most have relied either on coarse-resolution long-term datasets or on fine-scale case studies,

118

with limited integration across sensors. Second, although retrieved transition dates are known to be scale-

119

dependent, this has not been assessed in monsoonal deciduous forests, where cloud cover and revisit intervals

120

may govern how much of the seasonal cycle each sensor observes. Third, existing work often emphasizes pattern

121

detection without quantifying the relative influence of climatic drivers such as rainfall and temperature, making

122

it difficult to distinguish climate-driven variability, including drought response, from topographic effects. This

123

third gap is significant because climatic drivers, such as rainfall variability, temperature fluctuations, and extreme

124

events (e.g., droughts) play a critical role in shaping phenological timing and productivity (Gusain et al. 2026). In

125

monsoon-dependent ecosystems, even short-term rainfall deficits can significantly alter growing season length

126

and seasonal vegetation dynamics, often interacting with terrain-driven moisture gradients (Singh et al. 2020;

127

Singh et al. 2021; Seneviratne et al. 2021). Therefore, interpreting phenological anomalies requires linking

128

observed deviations not only to statistical metrics but also to underlying climatic conditions, particularly during

129

drought years and periods of monsoon variability.

130

The Bori Wildlife Sanctuary (BWS) in central India provides an ideal case study for investigating these dynamics.

131

Its heterogeneous landscape, comprising moist and dry deciduous forests distributed along steep topographic

132

gradients, functions as a natural laboratory for examining how seasonal timing, productivity, topography, and

133

climatic stress interact to shape phenology. Terrain influences phenology mainly through moisture availability, as

134

valleys retain soil moisture into the dry season, whereas uplands and steep slopes dry more quickly. The satellite

135

record spans 1 December 2018 to 31 October 2024, providing five complete July–June phenological years (2019–

136

2020 to 2023–2024) that form the basis of all analyses; the truncated 2018–2019 and 2024–2025 portions are

137

retained only for time-series continuity and are excluded from all statistics. Against this backdrop, the present

138

study formulates the following objectives. 1. Derive and assess consistency of seasonal phenological metrics

139

(SOS, POS, EOS, and Growing Season Length) and seasonal greenness metrics (Amplitude, Integral/AUC) from

140

NDVI and EVI time series, and assess their consistency across MODIS, Landsat-8, and Sentinel-2 datasets. 2.

141

Examine eco-topographic controls by analysing how elevation and slope gradients influence phenological timing

142

and seasonal greenness. 3. Detect interannual variations and climatic anomalies, particularly drought-related

143

disruptions, using breakpoint and anomaly detection methods to assess vegetation sensitivity and resilience. 4.

144

Synthesize findings for ecological and management implications, highlighting the role of multi-sensor approaches

145

in monitoring tropical deciduous forests under climate variability.

5

146

The individual components of this design, namely multi-sensor phenology, eco-topographic stratification, and

147

changepoint analysis, have each been deployed independently in prior research. The core novelty of this study lies

148

in the framework's integrated application to a tropical deciduous forest in central India. By simultaneously

149

extracting identical metrics across three spatial resolutions and three cloud-masking schemes, this approach

150

successfully decouples subtle interannual vegetation changes from sensor-specific artifacts and processing-chain

151

biases, a capability that single-sensor records inherently lack. By explicitly integrating multi-sensor observations

152

with eco-topographic analysis and climate-linked anomaly detection, this study aims to move beyond descriptive

153

phenology towards a more process-oriented understanding of vegetation dynamics in tropical deciduous systems.

154

It further demonstrates the value of integrating multi-sensor and multi-index datasets for robust monitoring,

155

providing insights that are directly relevant to ecological research, conservation planning, and climate adaptation

156

in central India and comparable ecosystems.

157

2. Study Area

158

The Bori Wildlife Sanctuary (BWS), located in the Hoshangabad District of Madhya Pradesh, central

159

India, covers approximately 486 km² and forms part of the larger Satpura Tiger Reserve (STR), which also

160

includes Satpura National Park (SNP) and Pachmarhi Wildlife Sanctuary (Rao et al. 2025). Geographically, BWS

161

lies between 22°19′N–22°30′N and 77°56′E–78°20′E (Government of Madhya Pradesh, Forest Department,

162

1977), bounded by the Tawa River in the north and surrounded by the rugged Satpura Range (Ramsar RIS; Key

163

Biodiversity Areas, n.d.). Elevations range from 300 to 1352 m above sea level, including prominent peaks such

164

as Dhoopgarh and Mahadeo, creating strong topographic and microclimatic gradients that influence vegetation

165

distribution and phenology (Satpura Tiger Reserve (n.d.), Figure 1).

166

Climatically, BWS experiences three distinct seasons: summer (March–June), monsoon (July–September), and

167

winter (October–February) (Weather & Climate - Satpura National Park, n.d.). Rainfall is strongly seasonal, with

168

most precipitation occurring during the southwest monsoon from late June to September. The CHIRPS

169

climatology used in this study indicates a mean June–September rainfall of 1460.8 mm for 1991–2020, providing

170

the climatic baseline for assessing interannual rainfall variability. The extended dry period from November to May

171

is marked by high temperatures and soil moisture deficits, contributing to widespread leaf shedding in deciduous

172

species (Singh et al. 2020). Seasonal transitions are therefore closely tied to monsoonal rainfall and moisture

173

availability, making the region highly sensitive to interannual climatic variability (White et al. 1997; Liu et al.

174

2021; Soni and Tripathi, 2024). Variability in monsoon behaviour, including delayed onset, intra-seasonal dry

175

spells, and early withdrawal, is a defining feature of the regional climate and has been shown to strongly influence

6

176

vegetation productivity and phenological timing (Singh et al. 2021; Seneviratne et al. 2021). This variability

177

provides an important context for interpreting short-term phenological anomalies observed during the study

178

period.

179

Geologically, the Bori–Pachmarhi landscape comprises Deccan Trap basalts with dolerite intrusions, transitioning

180

eastwards into Gondwana sandstones (Banerjee et al. 2022). These formations give rise to a dissected terrain of

181

plateaus and valleys with contrasting soil types, including well-drained loamy soils in forested uplands and black

182

cotton (regur) soils in lower-lying areas (RSIS 2024). Edaphic and topographic heterogeneity regulates water

183

retention and drainage, creating a spatial variation in moisture availability where valleys retain water for longer

184

durations and uplands or steep slopes experience rapid runoff, thereby directly driving vegetation dynamics

185

(UNESCO, n.d.).

Vegetation forms a mosaic of moist and dry deciduous forest types (Champion and Seth 1968), dominated by Sal

191

(Shorea robusta), Teak (Tectona grandis), and mixed deciduous species (Reserve Profile - Satpura Tiger Reserve,

192

n.d.). Species composition varies with moisture and terrain, with moist assemblages occupying higher elevations

193

and valley zones, and drier mixed forests distributed across uplands and slopes (Reserve Profile - Satpura Tiger

194

Reserve, n.d.). Riparian corridors along the Tawa River support relatively evergreen or semi-evergreen elements,

195

contributing to localized ecological buffering during dry periods (see Sunil et al. 2019 for T. arjuna as a riparian

196

dominant). The heterogeneous vegetation structure, including mixed canopies and understory variability,

197

introduces spatial complexity in satellite-derived spectral signals, particularly when using sensors with different

198

spatial resolutions (Gong et al. 2024). This makes BWS a suitable landscape for evaluating multi-sensor

199

approaches to phenological analysis across scales.

200

The sanctuary supports rich biodiversity, including key deciduous species such as Tectona grandis, Anogeissus

201

latifolia, and Diospyros melanoxylon (Mukherjee, 1984; Flora - Satpura Tiger Reserve, n.d.). These ecosystems

202

provide critical services including carbon storage (Raha et al. 2020), soil stabilization (Pandey et al. 2024), and

203

water regulation (Clark et al. 2021), while also functioning as an important ecological corridor within the central

204

Indian highlands (Sharma et al. 2013). The presence of riparian zones and moisture-retaining habitats further

205

enhances ecosystem resilience by sustaining vegetation activity during dry periods, thereby influencing spatial

206

patterns of phenological response under climatic stress (Khan et al. 2025).

3. Data and Methods

208

3.1 Satellite Data Sets

209

To investigate the phenological dynamics of BWS and address the study objectives, we employed a

210

multi-sensor framework that combined long-term temporal continuity with fine-scale spatial detail. Three

211

complementary satellite datasets formed the basis of our analysis. MODIS MOD13Q1 (Collection 6) offered 16-

212

day composites of NDVI and EVI at 250 m resolution, enabling detection of inter-annual and intra-annual

213

vegetation trends across the six-year study period (2018–2024) with the analysed phenological-year ranging from

214

2019–20 to 2023–24. Landsat-8 Operational Land Imager (OLI) Analysis Ready Data, with 30 m resolution and

215

a 16-day revisit interval, provided an intermediate perspective that bridged the gap between coarse and fine

216

resolution. Sentinel-2 MSI Level 2A, atmospherically corrected and processed at 10–20 m resolution with a near

217

five-day revisit, captured fine-scale heterogeneity across valleys, uplands, and riparian corridors. These sensors

218

ensured that broad-scale trends, landscape-level transitions, and localized variations in forest phenology were

219

systematically represented.

8

220

Differences in spatial resolution (10–250 m), revisit frequency, and sensor-specific radiometric characteristics can

221

introduce mixed pixel effects and scale-dependent variability, particularly in heterogeneous tropical landscapes

222

(Yan et al. 2025; Moon et al. 2021). An alternative to native-resolution analysis is spectral and geometric

223

harmonisation into a single reflectance series (Claverie et al. 2018; Bolton et al. 2020). However, the present

224

study did not adopt this approach because harmonisation combines sensors into a single product thereby removing

225

the cross-sensor contrast that this study uses as its consistency check. Rather than forcing spatial resampling,

226

which can introduce interpolation artefacts, each dataset was analysed at its native resolution to preserve sensor-

227

specific information content. Terrain-stratified comparisons are the exception because a terrain class is defined on

228

the 30 m SRTM grid, class membership for those comparisons is evaluated on a common 30 m grid, so that every

229

sensor is stratified by the same class boundaries. Consistency across sensors was subsequently evaluated using

230

statistical agreement metrics, allowing scale-related differences to be explicitly interpreted rather than suppressed.

231

To further address potential mixed-pixel and terrain representation effects, analyses were performed independently

232

within each sensor domain and subsequently compared, ensuring that observed differences reflect scale

233

characteristics rather than processing artefacts. In addition, terrain-wise comparisons were performed within each

234

sensor domain to avoid bias arising from unequal spatial representation across elevation and slope gradients. This

235

independent framework allowed us to empirically test and ultimately rule out spatial resolution as the primary

236

driver of varying terrain contrasts (see Section 4.1)

237

3.2 Vegetation Indices

238

NDVI and EVI were calculated from Sentinel-2 and Landsat-8 surface reflectance, whereas the

239

corresponding quality-screened layers were obtained directly from the MODIS MOD13Q1 product. The resulting

240

index time series were processed using a uniform temporal framework to enable cross-sensor comparison. NDVI,

241

based on red and NIR reflectance, provided sensitivity to canopy greening and senescence, while EVI

242

incorporating blue-band reflectance and atmospheric correction factors improved detection in dense canopies

243

where NDVI tends to saturate. The dual use of these indices was particularly valuable in BWS, where sparse dry

244

deciduous stands and closed-canopy moist forests coexist. By reducing ambiguities associated with canopy

245

structure, background soil reflectance, and non-photosynthetic vegetation in tropical systems (Huete et al. 2002;

246

Pettorelli et al. 2005), agreement between NDVI- and EVI-derived trajectories served as an internal consistency

247

check to enhance confidence in the reliability of the seasonal phenological transitions.

248

3.3 Preprocessing and Time-Series Smoothing

9

249

All imagery was processed on the Google Earth Engine platform, which facilitated standardized access

250

to global archives and ensured consistent application of preprocessing steps. Clouds and shadows were masked

251

using sensor-specific QA layers, non-forest areas were excluded with ESA WorldCover, and permanent water

252

bodies were removed using the JRC Global Surface Water dataset. To minimise cloud-related artefacts, only high-

253

quality observations flagged by QA bands were retained. Specifically, for Sentinel-2, the Scene Classification

254

Layer (SCL) was utilized to exclude classes 3 (cloud shadows), 8 (cloud medium probability), 9 (cloud high

255

probability), 10 (thin cirrus), and 11 (snow/ice), alongside the standard QA60 band. Landsat-8 observations were

256

filtered using the QA_PIXEL cloud and cloud-shadow bits, while MODIS data was screened via the SummaryQA

257

metric to retain only good-quality pixels. Pixels affected by clouds, cirrus, or shadows were excluded prior to

258

compositing. The spatial and temporal distribution of valid observations was quantified to ensure sufficient

259

seasonal coverage, particularly during the monsoon period when cloud contamination is highest. Composite

260

periods were retained only when they met the valid-pixel criterion defined in Section 3.6. No sensor–index–year

261

combination fell below the minimum number of observations required for a seasonal reconstruction, so none was

262

excluded from metric extraction. However, when peak-season coverage was lost, the affected metrics were

263

interpolated rather than observed and are identified as such.

264

To harmonize temporal coverage, Sentinel-2 and Landsat-8 images were aggregated into 14-day (fortnightly)

265

composites using the median, while MODIS was retained at its native 16-day composite interval. The choice

266

affects Sentinel-2 the most because it is the sole sensor with multiple acquisitions available within many

267

compositing periods. In contrast, Landsat-8 has fewer acquisitions, and MODIS has a single composite

268

representing each nominal 16-day interval, which is already supplied by NASA as a 16-day maximum-value

269

composite. Thus, in practice Landsat-8 and MODIS series remain unchanged by construction. Spatial reduction

270

across pixels within the sanctuary or terrain class was performed using the arithmetic mean throughout, as the

271

quantity of interest was landscape-averaged greenness. The compositing reducer acts across scenes, whereas the

272

spatial reducer acts across pixels.

273

EVI composites were screened for non-physical values arising from the index denominator approaching zero by

274

masking values outside the range (−0.2, 1.0), a convention applied in the MOD13Q1 quality specification. This

275

screening removed 16 Sentinel-2 composite values across 13 dates, resulting in the loss of the entire August–

276

September record for phenological year 2020–21, as one composite was absent due to cloud cover and three were

277

screened out as non-physical, leaving no valid peak-season observation for that sensor–index–year combination.

278

The corresponding amplitude estimate is interpolated rather than observed and is not interpreted. A comparable

10

279

loss affects Landsat-8 independently of the index: in phenological years 2019–20 and 2021–22 no August–

280

September Landsat-8 composite met the valid-pixel criterion of Section 3.6, so peak-season greenness in those

281

years is interpolated across the monsoon rather than observed. This follows from the 16-day revisit interval

282

coinciding with monsoon cloud cover rather than from the quality screen itself, and it is a further reason peak-

283

season quantities are reported per sensor rather than pooled. All remaining sensor–index–years retain at least two

284

valid peak-season composites.

285

Fortnightly compositing represents a trade-off between reducing noise due to cloud contamination and preserving

286

phenological signal integrity. In tropical deciduous systems where transitions occur over multi-week periods, such

287

aggregation has been shown to retain seasonal dynamics while improving temporal continuity (Zhang et al. 2003).

288

Phenological signals were extracted after smoothing of these composites, ensuring that aggregation did not

289

suppress key transition points but instead improved temporal continuity for curve fitting.

290

Vegetation index trajectories were smoothed using Gaussian Process Regression (GPR) to account for noise

291

arising from cloud contamination and irregular clear-sky observations. GPR was selected over spline interpolation

292

and Savitzky–Golay filtering (Chen et al. 2004) because it models temporal covariance structures, allowing

293

observations to be weighted according to temporal proximity. The GPR used a Radial Basis Function (RBF) kernel

294

with a white-noise component, and the same kernel specification and initialisation were applied across all sensor–

295

index series. Kernel hyperparameters (length scale, bounded between 5 and 400 days; noise level, bounded

296

between 10⁻⁷ and 1.0) were optimised independently for each series by maximising the marginal likelihood with

297

three restarts. Thus, the degree of smoothing varied between series rather than being fixed across them. GPR was

298

chosen because it can interpolate across irregular sampling intervals without requiring evenly spaced observations

299

and provides a posterior variance alongside the fitted mean. Smoothing attenuates short-duration fluctuations

300

while preserving the dominant seasonal signal required for metric extraction.

301

Fit quality is necessarily constrained by observation density. Within the green-up void window, the fraction of

302

fortnights with no valid observation was 33% for Sentinel-2, 59% for Landsat, and 51% for MODIS, increasing

303

to 78% in the high-elevation class (>650 m a.s.l.), where MODIS 250 m pixels rarely fall entirely within the class.

304

Maximum interpolated gaps were 56–84 days for Sentinel-2, 98–126 days for Landsat, and 84–126 days for

305

MODIS. Accordingly, metrics extracted across extended gaps are interpreted with caution. A schematic of the

306

end-to-end workflow is shown in Figure 2.

3.4 Phenological Metrics Extraction

319

Phenological metrics were then extracted from the smoothed NDVI and EVI series. Phenological metrics

320

and interannual comparisons were restricted to complete July–June phenological years (2019–2020 to 2023–

321

2024). The boundary year 2018–2019 and 2024–2025, which were only partially covered because of the initiation

12

322

of the study period, is shown only for contextual time-series visualization and was excluded from all metric-based

323

trend and statistical analyses. SOS and EOS were defined using a threshold criterion as the dates when index

324

values crossed 20% of the seasonal amplitude relative to the per-season baseline described below. A derivative-

325

based calculation was applied separately to the same fitted curves to obtain maximum green-up and brown-down

326

rates, which are reported in Supplementary Table S2; the derivative was not used to define the transition dates.

327

Although phenological metrics are sensitive to threshold selection, a moderate threshold of 20% has been shown

328

to provide stable estimates of seasonal transitions in tropical systems (White et al. 2009), and a sensitivity analysis

329

using 10%, 20%, and 30% thresholds is reported in Section 4.1.

330

Beyond SOS, EOS, and POS, additional indicators, including Growing Season Length (GSL), amplitude, and

331

Area Under the Curve (AUC) were computed to represent seasonal duration, seasonal range in canopy greenness,

332

and cumulative seasonal greenness respectively. These metrics provided the quantitative basis for subsequent

333

comparisons across years, and eco-topographic gradients. Although derived from spectral indices, these metrics

334

are widely used proxies for canopy development and phenological timing and have demonstrated strong

335

relationships with ecosystem functioning and climatic variability in comparable environments (Pettorelli et al.

336

2005; Piao et al. 2019). They are not, however, measures of productivity. Greenness and gross primary production

337

can decouple systematically along aridity gradients (Hu et al. 2022) and diverge in direction across large fractions

338

of the vegetated surface (Ding et al. 2020; Zhao et al. 2022), so the seasonal integral is reported here throughout

339

as a greenness metric and is not interpreted as carbon uptake. Peak of Season is retained for completeness;

340

however, because it occurs on a broad monsoon plateau rather than on a steep limb of the seasonal curve, its date

341

cannot be reliably determined and is therefore reported as a plateau period and excluded from statistical

342

comparison (Section 4.1).

343

Seasonal amplitude was computed against a per-season baseline rather than a single global minimum. For each

344

phenological year, the baseline was defined as the 10th percentile of index values within a 30-day window around

345

each flanking seasonal minimum, ensuring that a single anomalous composite could not determine the baseline.

346

Seasonal baselines ranged from 0.305 to 0.435 NDVI, compared with a global baseline of 0.317; this spread

347

represents roughly 29% of the seasonal amplitude, indicating that a single global baseline would suppress a

348

substantial portion of the interannual signal. This choice allows the dry season result in Section 4.3 to be detected.

349

All phenological metrics were computed from the actual acquisition timestamps. SOS is reported as calendar day-

350

of-year (DOY). EOS is computed using a phenological-year-anchored day count rather than calendar DOY

13

351

because EOS dates fall on either side of the December–January boundary, which would make adjacent dates

352

appear to be approximately 360 days apart on the calendar scale. For visualization and summary, these dates were

353

aggregated into 24 equal 15-day fortnight bins (F01–F24) anchored to the phenological year, with F01 = 1–15

354

July and any residual days merged into F24 (Supplementary Table S1). This fortnight axis wraps at the F24/F01

355

boundary and is used for display only; all measures of central tendency and dispersion are computed on calendar

356

day-of-year or on the non-wrapping phenological-day count.

357

3.5 Cross-Sensor Consistency Assessment

358

Given the reliance on multiple sensors, cross-sensor assessment was essential. Derived metrics were

359

compared across MODIS, Landsat-8, and Sentinel-2 using Spearman rank correlations on paired phenological

360

years, computed separately for each vegetation index so that sensor and index did not vary simultaneously. Two-

361

sided p-values were obtained by permutation rather than from the asymptotic approximation, which is unreliable

362

at this sample size. Because five paired observations yield only 120 unique rank permutations, the permutation

363

set was enumerated exhaustively rather than sampled, so the p-values are exact, and the resolution of the resulting

364

p-value is correspondingly limited to multiples of 1/120. Because rank correlation is insensitive to systematic

365

offsets, agreement was also quantified as the mean bias, mean absolute difference (MAD), and root mean square

366

difference (RMSE) between each sensor pair (Supplementary Table S4). These metrics characterize agreement

367

between measurement systems rather than accuracy, given that no individual sensor serves as a reference. The

368

methodological basis for matching observations across sensors, temporally for whole-sanctuary comparisons and

369

on a common 30 m grid for terrain-stratified comparisons, is described in Section 3.1.

370

These coefficients are reported as measures of agreement rather than as hypothesis tests because of the short record

371

length. With five paired phenological years, there are 120 possible rank permutations, giving a minimum attainable

372

two-sided exact p-value of 0.017 for an individual comparison. Consequently, an isolated comparison with p =

373

0.017 cannot survive Benjamini–Hochberg correction across the family of 30 cross-sensor comparisons. We

374

therefore emphasise the magnitude and direction of the rank correlations rather than statistical significance and

375

interpret them as measures of cross-sensor consistency rather than formal evidence of association.

376

In the absence of ground-based phenological observations, agreement across sensors and indices was used as an

377

internal consistency assessment. Convergence of SOS, POS, GSL, and seasonal greenness metrics across sensor-

378

specific datasets increases confidence that the extracted phenological signals are reproducible across measurement

379

systems rather than being specific to a single sensor or processing chain. This approach does not establish

14

380

agreement with field-observed phenology and is therefore interpreted as a consistency assessment rather than

381

validation. It is complemented by an assessment of ecological consistency with known climatic variability (Zhang

382

et al. 2006; Caparros-Santiago et al. 2021).

383

3.6 Eco-Topographic Stratification

384

To evaluate the influence of local terrain, phenological metrics were stratified using Shuttle Radar

385

Topography Mission elevation and slope data at 30 m (SRTMGL1). Here, eco-topographic control refers to the

386

structuring of vegetation seasonality by environmental gradients associated with terrain, rather than a direct effect

387

of terrain geometry itself. Elevation is therefore considered in relation to associated temperature and rainfall

388

gradients, whereas slope is considered in relation to differences in soil depth, runoff, and moisture retention. These

389

terrain-associated gradients may influence the magnitude and shape of seasonal vegetation dynamics without

390

necessarily altering the timing of phenological transitions. Elevation was grouped into three classes (0–450 m,

391

450–650 m, >650 m) representing valleys, mid-elevation forests, and upland plateaus, while slope was divided

392

into four categories (0–5°, 5–10°, 10–15°, 15–90°). These thresholds aligned with ecological transitions observed

393

in central Indian forests and allowed assessment of how topography mediated GSL, seasonal amplitude, and the

394

seasonal greenness integral.

395

Terrain classes were retained only where the class could be reliably measured. Class purity was assessed at each

396

sensor’s analysis resolution, with MODIS slope classes showing purities of only 0–23% at 250 m, indicating that

397

a single pixel could span several slope classes; MODIS was therefore excluded from all slope comparisons. A

398

valid-pixel criterion was applied to every composite period, within each terrain class and across the sanctuary as

399

a whole. A period was retained only when the number of valid pixels was at least 50 and at least 20% of the

400

maximum number observed for that class over the record, ensuring that class means were not driven by a small

401

number of observations. At 10 m and 30 m, the second condition was the binding criterion because the class

402

maxima were large. The MODIS moderate, steep, and mid-elevation classes fell below this threshold, with median

403

valid-pixel counts of 27, 27, and 36, respectively, and were therefore excluded from the terrain-stratified

404

summaries. Consequently, these class means include only Sentinel-2 and Landsat-8 data for both gradients.

405

Importantly, these exclusions were based on empirical measurement characteristics rather than being assumed

406

from nominal spatial resolution.

407

Two tests were applied to the stratified metrics. A Kruskal–Wallis test examined whether metric distributions

408

differed across terrain classes within each sensor. Because this test discards the class ordering implied by the

15

409

terrain hypothesis, a directional test was used as the primary analysis, namely, a Spearman rank correlation

410

between the ordinal class index and the metric, with exact p-values obtained by permutation. For each sensor–

411

index–gradient combination, the directional test used the individual sensor-year observations across terrain

412

classes, giving four terrain classes × five phenological years = 20 observations for slope and three terrain classes

413

× five phenological years = 15 observations for elevation, where all required class-year observations were

414

available. The terrain class index was assigned according to the ordered classes defined above. Both tests were

415

corrected for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure across the family of 20 terrain tests.

416

With five phenological years per class, the omnibus test has limited power; therefore, replication of the direction

417

of change across sensors and indices is treated as the interpretable evidence rather than any individual coefficient.

418

3.7 Rainfall Climatology, Anomaly and Changepoint Detection

419

Since one of our objectives was to detect climatic anomalies, we applied dedicated techniques to identify

420

disruptions in seasonal trajectories. Areal-mean daily precipitation over the sanctuary was extracted from CHIRPS

421

at 5566 m and aggregated to monsoon (June–September) and phenological-year totals. Anomalies and

422

standardised scores are referenced to the 1991–2020 climatological normal computed from the same product

423

(JJAS mean 1460.8 mm, SD 339.0 mm), rather than to a baseline drawn from the study period itself.

424

Interannual anomalies in vegetation metrics were quantified as z-scores of the seasonal greenness integral across

425

the five complete phenological years, with deviations of |z| ≥ 2 marking years of unusually low or high seasonal

426

greenness. Changepoint detection used the Pruned Exact Linear Time (PELT) algorithm with an exact L2 cost,

427

applied to the seasonal anomaly series, observed fortnightly NDVI minus the per-fortnight-of-year climatology,

428

instead of the raw trajectory, so that a detected changepoint represents a departure from the normal seasonal course

429

rather than the seasonal course itself. Each detected changepoint was then tested against CHIRPS monthly rainfall

430

for the break month and the preceding month, so that any attribution to rainfall could be stated as a tested condition.

431

3.8 Clustering of Phenological Regimes

432

Finally, to synthesize these seasonal dynamics, we applied an exploratory k-means clustering of multi-

433

year seasonal amplitude and greenness integral. Clustering was performed separately for NDVI and EVI using

434

class means pooled across the three sensors, yielding 35 observations per index (seven terrain classes × five

435

phenological years). The optimal number of clusters was determined using silhouette scores across k = 2–5, which

436

favoured a three-cluster solution (silhouette = 0.49 for NDVI). Cluster membership was then cross tabulated

437

against phenological year and terrain class. Because every expected cell count in these consistency tables was

438

below five, associations were assessed using permutation tests with 50,000 resamples rather than the asymptotic

16

439

chi-square approximation, which is invalid given this level of data sparsity. The clustering did not resolve eco-

440

topographic regimes. Cluster membership corresponded primarily to phenological year (NDVI χ² = 48.3, EVI χ²

441

= 42.7; permutation p < 0.001 in both cases) and showed no overall association with terrain class (χ² = 8.9 and

442

9.8; permutation p = 0.80 and 0.68, respectively). The analysis is therefore reported as exploratory and is not

443

interpreted as an eco-topographic result. Its implications for the record length required to resolve terrain regimes

444

are discussed in Section 5, and the cluster composition is shown in Supplementary Figure S3.

4. Results and Discussion

446

4.1. Extraction and Cross-Sensor Consistency of Phenological Metrics

447

The first objective was to derive accurate phenological metrics from multi-sensor time series and assess

448

their consistency across platforms. The fitted seasonal trajectories from which all metrics were extracted are

449

shown in Figure 3, and phase timing across sensors in Figure 4. Vegetation cycles in BWS followed a strongly

450

seasonal rhythm, with green-up preceding the monsoon and canopy activity sustained through it, and this

451

seasonality was consistently detected by Sentinel-2, Landsat-8, and MODIS, indicating strong seasonal control

452

on vegetation dynamics.

The convergence of seasonal phase timing across three independent satellite platforms, combined with agreement

466

between NDVI and EVI indices, provides convergent evidence for the internal consistency of the extracted

467

phenological metrics. In the absence of ground observations, such multi-sensor and multi-index consistency has

468

been widely used to assess the internal consistency of phenological retrievals in tropical ecosystems (Zhang et al.

18

469

2006; Caparros-Santiago et al. 2021), indicating that the observed seasonal patterns are reproducible across the

470

three measurement systems, although this consistency does not constitute independent validation against field

471

observations.

472

Both NDVI and EVI showed a clear SOS in late May (pooled mean calendar DOY 150.0 ± 19.2, range 129–182;

473

Table 1, Figure 4), preceding the onset of the southwest monsoon and consistent with a pre-monsoon green flush

474

that is not temporally aligned with monsoon rainfall onset (Singh et al. 2020; Soni and Tripathi 2024). This was

475

followed by a broad peak-of-season plateau spanning approximately August to November, during which canopy

476

density was at or near its maximum. Senescence was completed in February–March of the following calendar

477

year (EOS at calendar DOY 64.1 ± 11.2, range 43–88), marking the end of the growing season. GSL averaged

478

279.3 ± 18.8 days (range 253–311) and varied among years. Mean GSL differed among sensors, ranging from

479

269.0 days for MODIS to 291.6 days for Sentinel-2, but all three sensors captured a broadly similar seasonal

480

duration. This duration describes the interval over which sanctuary-mean greenness remains elevated above a 20%

481

amplitude threshold, not the leaf-on duration of an individual tree: landscape greenness in BWS does not fall to a

482

low dry-season value (baseline approximately 0.30–0.44 NDVI), because riparian and semi-evergreen elements

483

sustain the landscape mean through periods when much of the deciduous canopy is bare. Stand-level growing-

484

season durations reported for dry deciduous forests are therefore not directly comparable with the landscape-scale

485

threshold-derived GSL used here, and no direct comparison is attempted. Interannual variability in these seasonal

486

metrics highlights year-to-year shifts in SOS, EOS, and the seasonal greenness integral (Figure 5).

487

These transitions align with known eco-physiological behaviour of tropical deciduous forests, where monsoon-

488

driven moisture availability governs both canopy development and senescence timing (Gusain et al. 2026).

489

Because green-up occurs before the monsoon, the SOS transition is observed in clear-sky data ahead of the

490

seasonal cloud gap rather than reconstructed across it; therefore, a monsoon-period depression in NDVI or EVI is

491

unlikely to produce a false or delayed SOS in this record. Comparable satellite phenology assessments in other

492

Indian biodiversity hotspots report the same need to distinguish observational limitations from canopy behaviour

493

when interpreting monsoon-period retrievals (Bhuyan et al. 2025). Varying the amplitude threshold moves the

494

metrics systematically, as expected, pooled SOS advances from calendar DOY 160.7 at a 30% threshold to 150.0

495

at 20% and 135.5 at 10%, with EOS moving in the opposite direction, but the dispersion is almost invariant (SOS

496

SD 19.2–19.6 days across all three thresholds), and at the primary configuration zero of 240 class–year estimates

497

are flagged as boundary-affected. Varying the search buffer across 60, 90 and 120 days leaves the pooled metrics

498

unchanged to the reported precision.

no valid observation was 0.33 for Sentinel-2, 0.51 for MODIS, and 0.59 for Landsat-8, because Sentinel-2

584

acquired a median of 14 scenes per compositing period, compared with 2 for Landsat-8 and 1 for MODIS. Under

585

the monsoon-cloud conditions examined here, revisit frequency appears to be a stronger constraint than pixel size

586

on how much of the seasonal cycle is observed.

587

Agreement between sensors was assessed using Spearman rank correlations on paired phenological years,

588

computed separately for each vegetation index so that sensor and index did not vary simultaneously. For the start

589

of the season in NDVI, all three pairwise correlations were strong and positive: Landsat–Sentinel-2, ρ = 0.975;

590

MODIS–Sentinel-2, ρ = 0.900; and Landsat–MODIS, ρ = 0.821. For EVI, the corresponding comparisons yielded

591

ρ = 0.900, 0.900, and 1.000. End-of-season timing showed weaker but consistently positive agreement (NDVI, ρ

592

= 0.700–0.900), as did growing season length (ρ = 0.600–0.900). These coefficients are reported as measures of

593

agreement rather than as hypothesis tests, consistent with the rationale set out in Section 3.5: with five paired

594

phenological years, the discrete permutation space strongly limits inferential power, and no individual comparison

595

with the minimum attainable p-value of 0.017 can survive the applied multiple-comparison procedure when

596

considered in isolation. The correlations are therefore interpreted as measures of cross-sensor agreement rather

597

than formal hypothesis tests. The Landsat-8–MODIS correspondence for EVI start of season illustrates this point:

598

although the ranks agree perfectly (ρ = 1.000), the exact p-value remains 0.017, the minimum attainable value

599

given the permutation set. Reporting that no comparison survives correction would therefore reflect the limited

600

sample size rather than the degree of sensor agreement. Cross-sensor comparisons of phase timing (Figure 4),

601

together with the metric summary in Table 1, indicate that the primary phenological phases are consistent across

602

sensors, even when secondary metrics vary.

603

Absolute agreement statistics support this reading (Supplementary Table S4). For the NDVI SOS, the mean bias

604

between sensor pairs was 2.2–6.8 days and the RMSE was 5.3–9.9 days, which is two to four times smaller than

605

the interannual standard deviation of the metric (19.1–21.9 days; Table 1). Agreement was weaker for EOS and

606

GSL (RMSE of 7.2–16.5 and 12.9–24.0 days, respectively), while POS showed an RMSE of 19.0–45.0 days,

607

consistent with its treatment as a plateau. Rank and numerical agreement are distinct properties: for the EVI SOS,

608

Landsat-8 and MODIS agree perfectly in rank (ρ = 1.000), yet MODIS is earlier in every year by a mean of 6.0

609

days.

24

610

Further analysis showed that Landsat-8 placed EOS consistently earlier than Sentinel-2 in four of the five

611

phenological years, by a mean of 9.8 days (62.8 versus 72.6 calendar day-of-year), while MODIS tended to smooth

612

abrupt transitions. Despite these differences, SOS and GSL converged across sensors (SOS ranged from 146.2–

613

153.0 calendar day-of-year; GSL ranged from 269.0–291.6 days). POS did not converge and is reported as a

614

plateau rather than a date for that reason. Rate comparisons (Supplementary Table S2) show maximum green-up

615

rates of 0.0041–0.0049 NDVI units per day across the three sensors, with the corresponding brown-down limb

616

consistently shallower in magnitude. NDVI green-up rates exceed EVI rates in Landsat-8 and MODIS but not in

617

Sentinel-2, where the order reverses. MODIS returns the lowest NDVI green-up rate among the three sensors. The

618

difference may partly reflect its coarser spatial sampling, but green-up rate magnitude can also be influenced by

619

revisit frequency, compositing, cloud screening, radiometric response, and index dynamic range. The present

620

comparison therefore does not attribute the difference to a single sensor characteristic.

621

From a broader perspective, the integration of phenological timing, greenness metrics, and cross-sensor

622

comparisons offer three main insights. First, green-up precedes the monsoon, while canopy activity is sustained

623

through and beyond it, indicating that the seasonal cycle is not simply entrained to rainfall arrival but also exhibits

624

a degree of decoupling, with direct implications for how these forests may respond to shifts in monsoon timing.

625

Second, seasonal amplitude declines systematically along both the slope and elevation gradients, while the

626

seasonal greenness integral does not, indicating that terrain constrains the magnitude of seasonal greening without

627

proportionately constraining the seasonal cumulative signal. Third, the complementary strengths of NDVI and

628

EVI highlight the importance of using both indices in tandem as NDVI captures early canopy fluctuations while

629

EVI provides stability in dense forests. Collectively, these results provide a consistent basis for further examining

630

the role of topography and climatic anomalies in shaping phenological variability in BWS.

4.2. Influence of Eco-Topographic Gradients in Seasonal Amplitude

632

The second objective was to examine how elevation and slope regulate phenological dynamics in BWS

633

(Table 2, Figure 6), highlighting spatial controls on vegetation response. Elevation and slope emerged as

634

determinants of seasonal amplitude rather than of seasonal timing. Seasonal amplitude declines monotonically

635

from flat to steep terrain (0.421 to 0.357) and from low to high elevation (0.422 to 0.318), with this decline present

636

637

slope comparisons and from terrain classes failing the spatial adequacy criterion, so the inferential terrain

638

comparison was restricted to the eligible Sentinel-2 and Landsat-8 observations (Table 2). Phenological timing,

The separate elevation and slope gradients showed a consistent eco-topographic structuring of seasonal contrast.

682

Valleys and low-slope areas maintained the highest seasonal amplitude, whereas high-elevation and steep-slope

683

classes showed the lowest values, with phenological timing varying little across the gradients. Taken together,

684

upland and steep-slope forests exhibit a damped seasonal cycle on a comparable or slightly higher baseline giving

685

a flatter annual curve rather than a uniformly less green one. Since amplitude is the difference between seasonal

686

peak and seasonal trough, a reduced amplitude at an unchanged or higher integral implies that the trough, rather

687

688

in Section 4.3 from the temporal perspective, where interannual variation was found to be concentrated in the dry-

689

690

691

minimum as the locus of variation provides stronger evidence than either analysis alone. This pattern is consistent

692

with a greater non-deciduous fraction of canopy or understorey cover on upland and steeper terrain, which could

693

raise the dry-season floor while leaving peak-season greenness comparatively unchanged. However, the present

694

data cannot directly test this mechanism, and it is therefore not claimed as a causal explanation. This stratification

695

aligns with established ecological classifications of central Indian forests (Champion and Seth 1968), indicating

696

strong correspondence between phenological patterns and known ecological gradients.

697

698

699

700

701

record. Whether topography buffers vegetation against drought, for which valley and riparian systems are

702

plausible refugia (Costa et al. 2022; Singh et al. 2020; 2021), remains an open question that would require a record

703

containing a deficient monsoon to resolve.

729

The third objective was to evaluate how interannual variability and climatic anomalies relate to

730

vegetation phenology in BWS. Areal-mean CHIRPS rainfall over the sanctuary, referenced to the 1991–2020

731

732

4.4. Integrated Synthesis and Management Implications

854

The fourth objective was to synthesize results across datasets, indices, and analyses to derive broader

855

ecological and management insights. A temporal overview of interannual phenological dynamics (Figure 5) and

856

the terrain-class means of seasonal metrics (Table 2) together show that vegetation phenology in BWS varies

857

across eco-topographic gradients and between phenological years, while the available rainfall analysis does not

858

indicate a direct association between JJAS rainfall and the observed phenological metrics. Seasonal metrics (SOS,

859

POS, EOS, GSL) and greenness metrics (amplitude, seasonal integral) consistently captured pre-monsoon green-

860

up, an extended monsoon plateau, and dry-season senescence. Cross-sensor comparisons showed strong

861

agreement in SOS and GSL across Sentinel-2, Landsat-8, and MODIS, while EOS remained more variable and

862

POS, sitting on a broad plateau, was not resolvable as a date. This cross-sensor consistency indicates that the

863

observed seasonal dynamics are reproducible across the three satellite measurement systems.

864

Eco-topographic gradients emerged as controls on seasonal amplitude rather than on phenological timing. Valleys

865

and riparian forests sustained the largest seasonal contrast between dry-season trough and monsoon peak, while

866

upland and steep-slope forests showed a damped cycle, lower amplitude on a comparable or slightly higher

867

seasonal integral, with green-up and season length statistically indistinguishable across all terrain classes (Figure

868

6, Table 2). The gradient is therefore one of seasonal contrast, not total greenness: upland forests are not less green

869

overall. Because amplitude represents the difference between the seasonal peak and trough, and peak-season

870

greenness varies little across classes, the observed difference is primarily attributable to the dry-season floor,

871

which is the same locus of variation identified independently in the interannual analysis.

872

Anomaly and breakpoint analyses added temporal detail to this framework. Departures from the seasonal

873

climatology were modest in every complete phenological year, and all five detected changepoints were positive

36

874

shifts, with none attributable to a rainfall deficit (Figure 8, Figure 10). The substantive interannual signal instead

875

lay in the rise in dry-season baseline greenness over the final two years across all three sensors, without any

876

corresponding association with rainfall, suggesting that interannual variation in this forest is expressed more

877

strongly in the seasonal floor than in peak-season greenness. If sustained, a rising dry-season floor would alter

878

forage availability and fire regime, with consequences distinct from those of growing-season change. However,

879

given the five complete phenological years analysed, these implications should be interpreted as emerging patterns

880

rather than long-term ecosystem shift.

881

From a management perspective, three priorities emerge. First, low-elevation and low-slope forest areas may

882

warrant particular attention in conservation planning because they showed the greatest seasonal greenness contrast

883

and are the likely reservoirs of dry-season moisture in the landscape. Second, upland and steep-slope forests may

884

benefit from targeted soil and water conservation measures and systematic monitoring of changes in seasonal

885

greenness. Third, integrating multi-sensor, multi-index phenological monitoring into routine management could

886

contribute to future early-warning frameworks for detecting persistent changes in vegetation condition and

887

guiding adaptive responses. The increasing availability of near-real-time satellite data and open-access platforms

888

makes such approaches operationally feasible.

889

The methodological components used here are not individually novel: multi-sensor phenology, eco-topographic

890

stratification, and changepoint and anomaly analysis have each been applied in previous studies. The contribution

891

of this study lies in their combined application to a tropical deciduous forest system in central India, where the

892

multi-sensor design allows a small dry-season to increase in baseline greenness to be distinguished from potential

893

sensor or processing-chain effects. The reproducibility of this signal across three sensor systems provides a basis

894

for interpreting it as a consistent observation rather than attributing it to a single sensor.

895

Finally, the study reinforces phenology as both a scientific indicator and a management tool. By generating a

896

spatially explicit and temporally sensitive baseline of vegetation dynamics, it establishes a benchmark for

897

monitoring future climate-driven and land-use changes. This integration of remote sensing into conservation

898

planning supports informed decision-making and strengthens resilience in tropical deciduous forest systems.

899

5. Limitations and Future Directions

900

While this study demonstrates the feasibility of multi-sensor phenology monitoring for BWS, several

901

limitations remain. The absence of ground-based observations restricts direct validation of satellite-derived

902

phenological events, and the use of a static forest mask may introduce bias in heterogeneous or regenerating

37

903

stands. This is partly mitigated by strong agreement across sensors and vegetation indices, though such agreement

904

is corroboration rather than validation: the three products share a metric definition, a smoothing step and a

905

threshold rule, and can therefore agree while all departing from the surface in the same direction. From a

906

methodological perspective, GPR smoothing effectively stabilized noisy signals but may have attenuated short-

907

duration intra-seasonal events. This trade-off between noise reduction and signal sensitivity is inherent to time-

908

series smoothing and should be considered when interpreting fine-scale variability. A more fundamental constraint

909

is that the study period contains no drought. Relative to the 1991–2020 CHIRPS normal, no phenological year

910

reached the −19% deficit defining a deficient monsoon, and the two most anomalous years were wet. Drought

911

response therefore cannot be tested in this record, and the analysis instead characterises phenological behaviour

912

under non-deficient rainfall. Similarly, the terrain analysis identifies associations with elevation and slope classes

913

but does not isolate the underlying effects of temperature, soil depth, runoff, moisture retention, or vegetation

914

composition.

915

Statistical power is limited by record length. With five paired phenological years, the smallest attainable two-

916

sided exact p-value from a rank correlation is 2/120 = 0.017, so no cross-sensor comparison could survive

917

multiple-comparison correction across the family of thirty tests under any data. Rank coefficients are accordingly

918

reported as measures of agreement and the consistency of their direction, not as hypothesis tests. Peak of season

919

is subject to a related constraint, as it falls on a broad monsoon plateau where the location of the maximum is

920

determined by noise rather than by canopy state, and it is reported as a plateau period rather than as a dated event.

921

A key future need is in situ phenology observations across the elevation and slope classes used here, which would

922

advance the present consistency assessment into a true validation and enable the proposed causes of the dry-season

923

increase to be tested. Future work should address these gaps by extending the gridded rainfall analysis to

924

temperature and to sub-seasonal moisture indices, applying dynamic forest masks to capture canopy change, and

925

incorporating uncertainty propagation to quantify confidence in derived metrics. Developing quantitative climate–

926

phenology models will strengthen causal inference and improve predictive capability. Coupling these approaches

927

with harmonized Landsat–Sentinel time series and machine-learning methods can further enhance monitoring and

928

enable a shift toward near-real-time assessment of vegetation dynamics under climate variability.

929

6. Conclusions

930

This study shows that vegetation phenology in BWS is shaped by the combined influence of seasonal

931

moisture availability, topography, and interannual variability, and that the three operate on different components

38

932

of the seasonal cycle. Green-up occurs in late May, approximately five weeks before the monsoon arrives,

933

indicating a pre-monsoon flush that is not temporally aligned with monsoon rainfall onset, and the growing season

934

averages 279 days (range 253–311) at the landscape scale. Topography controls the amplitude of the seasonal

935

cycle, which declines monotonically from valleys to steep slopes and from low to high elevations, but not its

936

timing, which varies by less than three days across the full elevation gradient. Low-elevation and low-slope forest

937

classes therefore show the greatest seasonal contrast, while upland and steep-slope forests exhibit a damped

938

seasonal cycle on a comparable or slightly higher baseline rather than a system with uniformly lower seasonal

939

greenness.

940

The interannual signal in this record lies in the dry season rather than at the seasonal peak. Dry-season baseline

941

greenness rose over the final two phenological years by 0.058–0.066 NDVI on the raw measure and 0.098–0.109

942

on the fitted baseline, with the increase reproduced by all three sensors and uncorrelated with monsoon rainfall in

943

either the same or the preceding year. Given the five complete phenological years analysed and the confinement

944

of the baseline increase to the final two years, these results represent recent phenological dynamics and are better

945

described as a step change rather than a trend. Detecting this reproducible dry-season greenness increase, rather

946

than explaining its biophysical mechanism, is the principal contribution of this study; its consistent replication

947

across three independent sensors distinguishes it from a processing artifact, while its underlying cause remains an

948

area for future research.

949

The multi-sensor, multi-index approach enabled detection of both fine-scale variability and broader seasonal

950

patterns, including intra-seasonal anomalies. Consistent results across Sentinel-2, Landsat-8, and MODIS support

951

the cross-sensor reproducibility of the derived phenological metrics. Overall, this study provides a spatially and

952

temporally explicit baseline of vegetation dynamics and demonstrates the value of phenology for climate-

953

informed forest management. Incorporating topographic context and continuous monitoring can support targeted

954

conservation of resilient zones and adaptive management of vulnerable upland systems. These insights position

955

phenology as a practical tool for decision-making in tropical deciduous forests under increasing climatic

956

variability.

Figure S3: Composition of the three k-means clusters of seasonal amplitude and the seasonal
greenness integral (NDVI), by phenological year (left) and by terrain class (right), at a common
vertical scale. Clustering was performed on class means pooled across the three sensors, giving 35
observations (seven terrain classes × five phenological years), and separately for each vegetation
index. Cluster membership corresponds strongly to phenological year (χ² = 48.3, permutation p <
0.001) and shows no overall association with terrain class (χ² = 8.9, permutation p = 0.80); one
cluster comprises a single phenological year almost in its entirety. Permutation p-values are
reported because every expected cell count is below five. The low-amplitude, high-integral cluster
is dominated by 2023–24, the year with the highest dry-season baseline in the record, and every
one of its remaining members is an upland or steep-slope observation; this composition is reported
as an observation rather than as a test, since the clustering used amplitude and integral alone and
the composition was examined after the fact. Equivalent results for EVI are consistent throughout
(χ² = 42.7, p < 0.001 against year; χ² = 9.8, p = 0.68 against terrain class).
